White Hat: The Illusion of Parity

Dataset:The dataset used for this visualization was the raw Gender Equality Indicators 1960–2017 CSV file, gender-raw-2021.csv. Given that the file contained very extensive raw data, we cleaned the data to ensure that all countries in the drop-down filter had data for both education enrollment and employment. The visualization shows data from 2010-2019 with the slider becuase that time frame was when the most countries had data for the areas we were looking for. In order to ensure that we were able to visualize as much information as possible, we cleaned the data dynamically so the drop-down will change what countries are shown based on the year that is selected on the slider; only countries with data for that year will be part of the drop-down. Another thing to note with our dataset is that since gross enrolment values are used, it is possible for the enrolment rates for primary to exceed 100% because the way the metric in the dataset is calculated is that the total number of children enrolled is divided by the number of children that are supposed to be enrolled, and it is possible that the number of children enrolled is higher than the expected value.

Design Choices:The Dumbbell plot specifically was chosen in order to show gaps between men and women in the two sectors, educational enrollment and employment. This way, it would be clear how much the difference between the two is and how it changes as the level of education increases and similarly with types of employment. Keeping in mind that green and red are most commonly the two colors that are difficult to distinguish for those who are colorblind, we chose to use blue for men and orange for women as in the legend next to the title. We also used tooltips to make it clearer what the exact percentages were and what the gap was for each category when the area near that "dumbbell" is hovered over, as well as the overall employment rate. Zooming was another important design choice and feature we included becuase for some years and countries, the gaps are quite small, so to ensure users could clearly see the gap, we made it so you can zoom to more closely inspect the visualization.

Black Hat: Women's Workforce Momentum

Manipulative Techniques Used

This chart uses the same labor force participation data as our white hat employment view. It leads readers to believe that women are rapidly closing the workforce gap. In reality, the global gap barely moved between 2010 and 2019, staying at about 27 percentage points, and women's participation worldwide actually fell by about 1.5 points. Our techniques fall into all three rubric categories.

Data Transformation: We show percent change in the participation rate since 2010 instead of percentage point change. Women start from a lower base, so the same real gain looks much bigger. Turkey's women gained about 7 points but the chart shows growth of nearly 27% and that outlier pulls up the average growth stat. We also cherry-picked the data by only showing the nine G20 members where women's participation rose and left out the rest. In India, China, United States, and Russia women's participation dropped by about 1 to 5 percentage points. The World aggregate is also left out because it would contradict the headline.

Marks and Encodings: The bars only show growth from a 2010 baseline, so the actual participation rates never appear. Readers can't see that women in these countries still participate far less than men. Hovering over a bar only shows the growth since 2010, so the real rates stay hidden. Sorting the bars by women's growth puts the biggest orange bars at the top so the first impression is strong progress.

Titles and Labels: The headline, "Women Are Entering the Workforce at a Record Pace," turns small changes into a dramatic story. The subtitle says "G20 economies" implies that the whole group is shown when only a subset is. The headline stats which show women's average growth far ahead of men's and every economy rising, present the cherry-picked results as an overall verdict.

Design & Credibility: The chart copies the white hat's colors, fonts and legend, so it looks like part of the same honest report. It uses a zero-based axis, gridlines, a clear legend and a real source line, which are all signs of good practice. Every number on it is accurate, and the source line even states "percent change in participation rate," though most readers will read that as percentage points. The year slider makes the chart feel open to exploration, but every year uses the same countries and baseline, so readers always reach the same conclusion. Spotting the tricks takes checking which G20 countries are missing and knowing the difference between percent and percentage-point change.

Impact: Someone could use this chart to argue that workplace gender policies are no longer needed. Each choice can be defended on its own (Ex: "we focused on major economies," "percent change is standard") which is what makes the deception hard to challenge.
